# Research: how a seatbelt retractor locks

## What is depicted
An emergency locking retractor (ELR) with a **webbing-sensitive** lock, the kind the narration describes:

1. The belt is wound on a spring-loaded spool, so it pays out and reels in freely at normal speeds.
2. An inertia member rides on the spool's shaft and is carried round by a light spring.
3. When webbing is pulled out with a sudden acceleration, the spool jumps ahead and the inertia member lags behind it.
4. That lag drives a pawl outward into ratchet teeth, and the spool cannot turn any further in the pay-out direction.
5. When the pull is released, the spring returns the pawl and the belt moves freely again.

The cutaway shows exactly these parts: spool and webbing roll, lock carrier on the spool, pawl with return spring, inertia
disc with its cam pin, and the fixed ratchet teeth in the frame's side plate. The tooth shape lets the pawl catch in the
pay-out direction only.

## What the script simplifies (flagged, wording unchanged)
- **Two triggers exist.** Besides the webbing-sensitive lock, retractors have a **vehicle-sensitive** lock: a ball or pendulum
  that moves when the car decelerates sharply or tilts, and drives a pawl into the ratchet. Most modern retractors have both,
  and in a real crash the vehicle sensor usually acts first. The line "designed to lock when the belt gets pulled too
  suddenly" is true of the webbing-sensitive lock, and it is the one you feel when you yank the belt, but it is not the only
  thing that locks a belt in a crash. The video shows only the webbing-sensitive mechanism and never claims it is the only one.
- **"Catches you instantly."** A locked belt still lets the occupant move a little: the standard allows up to 25 mm of webbing
  before lock-up, and webbing stretches. The with-lock shot shows a short lurch and a rebound, not a frozen dummy.
- **"Much safer."** The comparison shows restrained versus unrestrained forward travel. It does not suggest a belt prevents all injury.
- Pretensioners and load limiters are part of modern belts and are not shown.

## Figures
- US standard FMVSS 209: an ELR must lock before 25 mm of webbing pays out at 0.7 g, and must not lock at 0.3 g of webbing
  acceleration (so normal movement stays free).
